Odoo is a good product, and most articles with this title will not tell you that. If your business runs the way Odoo expects, it is very hard to beat: mature, broad, cheap to start, and live in weeks. The businesses that end up searching for an alternative are rarely disappointed by its features. They are usually paying, month after month, for the gap between how the software works and how they actually work. This article is about identifying that gap early, because it is the only thing that decides which side of the build-or-buy line you belong on.

## Where Odoo genuinely wins

Be fair to it before you replace it.

**Breadth.** Accounting, inventory, purchasing, CRM, manufacturing, HR, ecommerce, point of sale: one vendor, one data model, hundreds of modules. Commissioning that breadth as a custom build would be irresponsible for most businesses.

**Maturity.** Every edge case in a general ledger, every tax rounding rule, every stock-valuation method has been hit by someone else already and fixed. A new build re-learns some of those lessons at your expense.

**Speed and first-year cost.** You can be running next month for the price of a subscription. A custom build is a project with a timeline.

**An exit.** The community edition is open source and the database is PostgreSQL. Your data is not hostage.

If you read that list and nothing troubles you, install Odoo. You do not need us.

## Where it starts costing you

**Per-user pricing compounds.** A subscription is charged per user, per month, forever, and it grows with your headcount. That is fine at eight users. At forty, in year four, it is a permanent line item that has bought you no asset. The number worth running is the three-year total against a one-time build plus maintenance.

**Customisation is where the money actually goes.** The licence is rarely the expensive part. Implementation partners, custom modules, and the ongoing work of keeping those modules compatible through version upgrades usually cost a multiple of the subscription. Businesses often discover they commissioned custom development anyway, only inside someone else's framework, and they still pay rent on it.

**The last 20% of your workflow.** Odoo models the average business in your industry. Your business is not average, which is usually why it is still in business. The parts that make you different (how you price for a particular dealer, how a credit hold is released, how a return is graded before it goes back on the shelf) are exactly the parts that end up in a spreadsheet beside the ERP. Every spreadsheet beside an ERP is a workflow the ERP failed to hold.

**Upgrade friction.** The more you customise, the more each major version becomes a project rather than a click. Some businesses solve this by simply not upgrading, which quietly turns a maintained product into unmaintained software.

## The question that actually decides it

Not "which has more features". This one:

> **Is the way we work a problem to be standardised, or an advantage to be protected?**

If your operation is messy because nobody ever designed it, an [off-the-shelf ERP](/insights/custom-software-vs-off-the-shelf) is a gift: it imposes a sane process and you should accept it. Adopt the standard and change your habits.

If your operation is deliberate — if the sequence you use is why customers stay with you, or why your margin is better than the shop down the road, then bending it to fit a template is not a saving. It is throwing away the thing you are competing on.

## A test you can run this week

Write down the five things your team does most often. For each, ask: could a competent stranger do it inside a standard ERP, using only its built-in screens?

- **Five yeses.** Buy the off-the-shelf suite. You will be live in a month and it will fit.
- **Three or four yeses.** Buy it, and accept that one or two things will live in a spreadsheet. That is a fair trade.
- **Two or fewer.** You are heading for heavy customisation. Price a purpose-built system in the same conversation, because you are going to pay for custom development either way, and one of the two options leaves you owning it.

## What a custom system actually buys

Not more features. Three specific things:

**Your process, not a configured approximation of it.** The credit check happens where your business puts it. The approval rule is your approval rule. Nobody trains a new hire on the workarounds.

**A cost that stops.** A build is a project with an end. Afterwards you pay for hosting and maintenance, commonly estimated at roughly 15–20% of build cost per year, which does not scale with headcount. Add twenty staff and the bill does not move.

**Ownership.** The source code, the database, the deployment. No vendor can change the pricing model or deprecate a module you depend on.

And the honest cost side: you carry the responsibility for maintenance, you wait for a build instead of a download, and you do not get a module library to grow into for free. That is the real trade.

## What it costs

There is no single number, and anyone quoting one before asking about your workflow is guessing. As a rough market picture: a focused operational system covering orders, inventory, invoicing and basic accounting for one business generally lands in the low tens of thousands of dollars; a multi-branch platform with purchasing, warehouses, double-entry accounting and reporting runs higher; anything with heavy integrations goes further again. What moves the number is module count, integrations, and how many people need to be in it at once.

The comparison worth making is not licence versus project fee. It is **three-year total** on both sides — subscription plus implementation plus customisation plus upgrade work, against build plus hosting plus maintenance.

## The short version

Odoo is not the wrong answer. It is the right answer for a business whose workflow is standard, and an expensive answer for a business whose workflow is its advantage. Decide which of those you are before you decide what to buy, and run the three-year number.
